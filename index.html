Who I Am

I am David P. Adekunle — a Nigerian geographer, research and education writer, and author. My work sits at the intersection of place, people, and policy, drawing from the rich and complex landscape of Nigerian society to produce writing that informs, challenges, and inspires.

Geography, for me, is not merely a discipline — it is a lens through which I read the world. Every map tells a story. Every spatial pattern reflects a decision, a history, a power structure. My goal is to translate these stories into accessible, rigorous, and meaningful narratives.

What Drives Me

Nigeria is one of the most geographically, demographically, and culturally complex nations on earth. Yet it is too often reduced in academic and public discourse to its problems. I believe in writing that restores complexity, honours nuance, and demands better from our institutions.

Education is at the heart of my research interests — not just schooling, but the broader question of how knowledge is produced, distributed, and denied in Nigerian society. Who gets to learn? Who gets to write? Who gets to be heard?

My Background

My academic and professional background is rooted in geography and the social sciences. I have developed expertise in research methodology, spatial analysis, GIS mapping, academic writing, and education commentary. I am equally comfortable in the field, in the library, or at the keyboard — translating data into insight and insight into impact.

Long-Term Vision

"To become one of Nigeria's foremost geographic voices — a researcher, writer, and author whose work shapes how Nigeria understands itself and how the world understands Nigeria."

EducationMay 2026

The State of Education in Nigeria: A Geographic Perspective

Nigeria's education crisis is not just a funding problem — it is a spatial problem. Schools are not distributed equally across the country, and the gap between urban and rural access tells a story that statistics alone cannot capture.

The Spatial Divide

When we map school distribution across Nigeria's 36 states, a stark pattern emerges. The South-West, particularly Lagos and Ogun states, enjoys significantly higher school density than the North-East or North-West. This is not merely a reflection of population distribution — it reflects decades of differential investment, colonial legacy, and political neglect.

Beyond Enrollment Numbers

Federal government data often cites enrollment figures as evidence of educational progress. But enrollment is not education. A child enrolled in a school with no furniture, no textbooks, and an absent teacher is not receiving an education — they are occupying a building that qualifies as a school on paper.

What Geography Tells Us

Geographic analysis of educational outcomes reveals something important: the farther you are from an urban centre in Nigeria, the lower your educational attainment is likely to be. This is true controlling for income, ethnicity, and religion. Space itself — distance, terrain, infrastructure — is an independent variable in educational inequality.

This has profound implications for policy. Education reform that does not account for geography is education reform that will fail the children who need it most.

Youth Unemployment and the Geography of Opportunity in Nigeria

Where you are born in Nigeria largely determines what opportunities are available to you. The concentration of economic activity in Lagos and Abuja, while vast regions remain underserved, is not an accident — it is a policy outcome.

A Tale of Two Nigerias

Nigeria's youth unemployment rate hovers above 40% by most credible estimates. But this national figure obscures a geographic reality: youth in rural areas and in the North face unemployment rates that dwarf even this sobering average.

The Infrastructure Connection

Opportunity follows infrastructure. Roads, electricity, internet connectivity, financial services — these are the foundations on which economic activity is built. Their uneven distribution across Nigerian space is perhaps the most consequential geographic fact of contemporary Nigerian life.

Addressing youth unemployment without addressing the spatial concentration of infrastructure is addressing symptoms while leaving the disease untouched.

Housing Crisis in Nigerian Cities: Who Is Building for Whom?

The Nigerian housing market is one of the most glaring examples of spatial injustice in contemporary Africa. As luxury estates multiply in Lekki and Maitama, millions remain in informal settlements with no security of tenure.

The Numbers

Nigeria has a housing deficit estimated at 28 million units. This number has grown steadily over the past two decades despite — or perhaps because of — various government housing schemes. The question is not whether Nigeria is building, but who it is building for.

Informal Settlements as Geography

Informal settlements are not planning failures — they are planning responses. When formal housing is priced beyond the reach of the majority, people create their own spatial solutions. Ajegunle, Makoko, and hundreds of similar communities across Nigerian cities represent the geographic ingenuity of people systematically excluded from the formal city.

Any serious housing policy must start by recognising this and engaging with informal settlements as legitimate urban space, not problems to be erased.
